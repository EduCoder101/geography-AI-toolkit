How AI Actually Works

A quick reference for geography teachers. What LLMs do, why they produce geography slop, and how to work with them rather than against them.

Understanding AI for geography

This page is a quick reference you can return to, not something you need to read start to finish. Each card covers one concept.

The geography slop example is worth sharing with colleagues or using as a staff meeting discussion starter. The contrast boxes (what AI does well vs. what it does not) are worth copying into a document or slide for classroom display or staff discussion.

AI is a prediction engine, not a knowledge engine

Large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini work by predicting the next word in a sequence. This is a simplification of a more complex process, but the key point stands: the model has no ground truth to check against. It generates the most statistically probable continuation of your prompt — that is, the words most likely to follow based on patterns in language it has seen before — not the most accurate one. When you ask a question, the AI is not looking up an answer. It is generating the sequence of words most likely to follow your prompt, based on those patterns learned during training.

This is a crucial distinction. The AI has absorbed an enormous amount of geography content through training, but it has no way to distinguish what is accurate from what merely sounds plausible. It cannot check its own outputs against reality. It can produce fluent paragraphs about urbanisation, coastal management, or climate change because those patterns appear frequently in its training data (the vast body of text the model learned from during development). But it has no understanding of spatial relationships, no mental model of how processes operate across scales, and no ability to evaluate whether the evidence it generates is geographically appropriate.

What AI does well

Produces fluent, well-structured text. Follows instructions about format and tone. Summarises and reorganises information. Generates ideas and brainstorms. Provides definitions and explanations of established concepts. Identifies patterns in text you give it.

What AI does not do

Understand spatial relationships. Reason about why processes operate differently in different places. Evaluate competing evidence or apply geographical judgement about which evidence is contextually appropriate to a specific place or argument. Generate genuinely new geographical analysis. Think about scale, interconnection, or environmental change the way a geographer does.

The implication for geography: AI can produce text that reads like geography, uses correct terminology, and follows expected structures. But it cannot do what geography actually requires: spatial reasoning, place-based analysis, and evidence-based argument. This gap is the source of most problems when AI meets the geography classroom.

What is geography slop?

Geography slop is AI-generated text that performs the surface features of geographical writing without demonstrating genuine geographical thinking. It uses the right words, follows the expected format, and would satisfy a marking rubric that rewards terminology, coverage, and structure. But something important is missing.

Geography slop does not reason spatially (it describes processes without connecting them to specific places). It does not move between scales (it stays at one level of analysis). It does not use evidence to support a position that could have gone the other way (it presents conclusions as inevitable). And it does not show the writer's own analytical decisions (which evidence to foreground, which framework to apply, which competing explanation to reject).

Example: Geography slop about urbanisation in Lagos
"Urbanisation in Lagos has had significant environmental and social impacts on the city's residents. Environmentally, the rapid growth of the city has led to increased air pollution from traffic congestion and industrial activity, as well as water pollution in the Lagos Lagoon from untreated sewage and industrial waste. Socially, the growth of informal settlements such as Makoko has led to overcrowding, with limited access to clean water, sanitation, and healthcare."
What is present: Correct terminology, named places (Makoko, Lagos Lagoon), environmental and social categories, fluent writing at an appropriate level.

What is absent: No spatial reasoning (why do these impacts manifest in these specific locations?). No scale connection (how do local impacts connect to national migration patterns or global economic forces?). No evidence weighed against alternatives (is overcrowding the most significant impact, or is it spatial inequality? The paragraph does not take a position). The specificity is cosmetic: Makoko and the Lagos Lagoon are the two most commonly cited examples in any text about Lagos.

The rubric test: If your marking criteria reward "identifies environmental and social impacts with geographical terminology and reference to a case study," this paragraph scores well. If your criteria require "evaluates impacts by weighing evidence and connecting to the specific physical and human geography of the megacity," it falls short. Geography slop exposes what the rubric actually values.

AI makes things up

Because LLMs generate text based on statistical patterns in language — which words tend to follow which other words in what kinds of context — rather than verified knowledge, they sometimes produce claims that are plausible-sounding but false. This is called hallucination. In geography, this is particularly dangerous because AI will fabricate statistics, conflate events from different places or time periods, invent case study details that sound specific but are not verifiable, and confidently describe geographical processes in ways that are subtly wrong — all in the same fluent, authoritative tone it uses for accurate information.

The practical rule: Hallucination is not evenly distributed. Conceptual explanations (how a delta forms, what drives rural-urban migration) are relatively reliable. Specific statistics, dates, citations, and granular case study details are where fabrication is most common and most confident-sounding. These are the claims that always warrant independent verification. If an AI output includes a named source, check that the source exists and says what the AI claims it says. This is especially important for geography resources that will be shared with students.

The cognitive co-worker framing

The most productive way to think about AI in geography is as a cognitive co-worker: a tool that extends your professional capability without replacing your professional judgement.

A cognitive co-worker can brainstorm ideas, draft text you will edit, organise information into structures, suggest approaches you had not considered, and give feedback on work you have already produced. What it cannot do is make the professional decisions: which evidence matters, which geographical framework fits, whether the analysis is spatially grounded, and whether the output is accurate.

This framing applies equally to teachers using AI to build resources and students using AI to support their learning. The question is never "should we use AI?" It is: "who is doing the geographical thinking?"

What this means for your geography classroom

Three practical implications follow from understanding how AI works:

1. Your prompting matters. Because AI generates the most likely response to your prompt, vague prompts produce generic output. Specific, geography-grounded prompts produce better results. The Prompt Pathway shows you the difference.

2. Your students need to see geography slop. If students do not know what AI-generated geography looks like, they cannot evaluate it. Showing them the contrast between genuine geographical thinking and geography slop builds critical literacy. The student prompt table can include examples of AI doing the thinking for the student as a teaching tool.

3. Your task design is your best defence. Speed bumps in question design help, but the reliable protection against students using AI to do their thinking for them is structural: in-room work, primary data, process evidence, and oral verification. The Task Design Principles page explains the framework.
