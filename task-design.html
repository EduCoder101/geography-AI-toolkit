Task Design Principles

A framework for designing geography tasks that work with AI rather than against it. Honest about what protects assessment integrity and what just makes the task better.

The design challenge

Almost any written component of a geography task can be produced by an AI tool. Rewriting questions to require comparison, evaluation, or place-specific reasoning raises the bar, but a student who wants to use AI to do their thinking can prompt their way through sophisticated analytical tasks almost as easily as simple descriptive ones.

This means the question is not "how do I make my task AI-proof?" It is: "where do I want to invest my effort, and what does each change actually buy me?"

The framework below gives you a structured way to make those decisions for any geography task.

The permit–curate–validate framework

Three steps for integrating AI into geography assessment. Each step serves a different purpose. The first two improve the task. The third protects its integrity.

1
Permit
Identify where AI use is appropriate and guide it with purposeful prompts.
2
Curate
Require students to transform, evaluate, and connect information rather than reproduce it.
3
Validate
Verify that the student genuinely understands the geographical thinking in their submission.

Step 1: Permit

Not all AI use is a problem. Some stages of a geography task genuinely benefit from AI support: clarifying unfamiliar terminology, brainstorming initial ideas, getting feedback on a draft structure, or checking whether a case study source actually exists.

Trying to ban AI from these stages is both unenforceable and pedagogically counterproductive. Instead, explicitly permit AI use where it adds value and guide students toward purposeful prompts that keep the geographical thinking with them.

The student prompt table generator produces guidance for exactly this: showing students how to use AI at each stage of a task in a way that supports their learning rather than replacing it.

The principle: When you permit AI use explicitly and guide it well, you reduce the incentive to use it secretly and badly. Students who know how to use AI as a thinking partner are less likely to use it as a shortcut.

Step 2: Curate and process

This is where you design the task so that students must do something with information that an AI cannot do for them: apply it to a specific local context, connect it to their own fieldwork data, weigh it against conflicting sources, or use it to support an argument they have independently constructed.

Curate-level changes improve the geographical thinking your task demands. They make the weakest AI outputs visibly inadequate. But be honest with yourself: most curate-level redesigns are speed bumps, not walls. They make the task better geography regardless of AI, but they do not prevent a determined student from prompting their way through.

That is still worth doing. Better geography is better geography. Just do not mistake a speed bump for a structural protection.

Step 3: Validate understanding

This is where assessment integrity actually lives. For any task where the final product is a written document submitted digitally, in-room, in-person, or process-based verification is the only reliable defence.

Validation means confirming that the student can demonstrate the geographical thinking their submission claims. This does not need to be a formal assessment. A 2-minute oral check-in, a quick question during class, a requirement to present one finding to a small group, or a timed in-class writing component all provide evidence that the student owns the work.

The goal is not to catch students out. It is to create conditions where genuine engagement with the task is the path of least resistance.

The three tiers of protection

Every change you make to a geography task falls into one of three tiers. Knowing which tier a change belongs to helps you invest your limited time where it matters most.

Speed bumps

Improve the task, raise the bar

Speed bumps make AI-generated output slightly less convincing and improve the geographical thinking the task demands. They are worth doing because they make the task better pedagogy. But a capable student with a capable AI tool can clear these hurdles with a few well-crafted prompts.

Geography examples: Requiring students to rank factors by significance rather than just list them. Anchoring an impact evaluation to the specific physical geography of the case study location. Adding a comparative element to a management strategy proposal. Requiring spatial analysis to reference specific features on an included map.
Structural protections

Require something AI cannot produce

Structural protections are the reliable defences. They require the student to contribute something that an AI genuinely cannot generate: primary data they physically collected, work performed in the room under observation, sustained process evidence maintained across weeks, or integration of direct personal experience.

Geography examples: Fieldwork data with raw data sheets, site photographs, and GPS logs. Hand-annotated maps with analytical observations (not just labels). In-class timed writing of one task component (20 minutes, open notes, no devices). A research journal maintained across multiple lessons showing how the student's thinking evolved.
Detection layers

Make it visible when AI has done the thinking

Detection layers do not prevent a student from using AI to do their thinking, but they make it visible when it has happened. They shift the risk: producing the submission is easy, but surviving the follow-up is not.

Geography examples: Source annotations (2–3 sentences per source explaining what was used and where it appears in the report). Oral check-ins where students explain one aspect of their work without notes. Submission of a draft alongside the final product. Small-group presentation of findings with a peer question.
Worked example

Year 9 urbanisation report

A typical Stage 5 Geography task: students select a megacity, research push/pull factors, analyse spatial patterns, evaluate impacts, and propose a management strategy. 1,000-word report, three sources, one annotated map.

Without the framework: Every written component is producible by AI at a convincing Year 9 level. The annotated map is the only element that current AI tools handle poorly. A student could submit a largely AI-generated report that would be difficult to distinguish from genuine work.

With the framework applied:

Speed bump Anchor the impact evaluation to the specific physical geography of the chosen megacity (Jakarta's subsidence, Dhaka's floodplain, Lagos's lagoon system). This makes generic AI output visibly inadequate and improves the geographical thinking.

Speed bump Require the management strategy to be evaluated against a named alternative, with justification for why one is more appropriate for the specific city.

Detection layer Add source annotations: 2–3 sentences per source explaining what the student used and where it appears in their report. Zero extra marking load; exposes fabricated references immediately.

Structural protection Specify that the annotated map must be hand-drawn or hand-annotated, with annotations that explain spatial patterns rather than just label features.

Structural protection Add one lesson of in-class timed writing for the management strategy section (20 minutes, open notes, no devices). A student who genuinely did the research will find this straightforward. A student who has not engaged with the task will struggle to reproduce the reasoning.

The key insight: The speed bumps make the task better geography. The detection layer makes it harder to sustain the pretence. The structural protections are what actually confirm the student can demonstrate geographical thinking independently. All five changes together take about 15 minutes to add to the task design and one lesson of class time to implement.

Where to start

You do not need to implement all three tiers at once. If you can only make one change to an existing task, here is the priority:

1. Add a detection layer. Source annotations are the highest-value, lowest-effort addition. They cost nothing to implement and expose the most common form of AI misuse (fabricated or unread references) immediately.

2. Add one structural protection. An oral check-in (2 minutes per student during class) or a timed in-class writing component (one lesson) provides direct evidence that students can demonstrate their geographical thinking independently.

3. Improve the task design. Speed bumps make the task better geography. Add them when you have time to rework the brief, but do not mistake them for security.

To audit a specific task against this framework, use the AI vulnerability critique prompt. It will classify every component of your task into the three tiers and suggest specific changes.
